# Assignment 1. What is something you believe to be true that most people around you would disagree with?

### Rules

* No "safe" answers.
* Must be your real belief (not copied from internet).
* Minimum 50 words.

**Hint:** What do you believe about career, money, learning, discipline, relationships, health, success, life, tech industry, etc. that most people don't agree with?

## Answer

Most people believe hard work guarantees success, but I think strategic work beats raw effort. I've seen people work 70-hour weeks with nothing to show for it, while others achieve more in 30 hours by choosing high-leverage activities. Hard work on the wrong things is just, hard. Smart work means identifying what actually moves and intellegence decisions in your field, then executing that repeatedly. People romanticize the grind because it feels virtuous, but results come from working on the right things, not just working more.

# Assignment 2. What are the top 3 objective truths you discovered through experimentation and results?

### Definition

Objective truths do not depend on opinions. They hold true regardless of how people feel.


---

## Truth #1

### Truth : 

Consistency compounds results more than natural talent.

### Evidence from my life

I came from a non-technical background but spent one year consistently learning and building projects while working full-time. Within 12 months, I went from knowing nothing to becoming proficient enough that people now ask me for technical advice. The consistency mattered more than any initial advantage—I beat people who were naturally smarter but inconsistent.

---

## Truth #2

### Truth

Comparing yourself to others destroys progress; comparing yourself to your past self reveals it.

### Evidence from my life

When I compare my skills to peers, I feel stuck (there's always someone better). When I compare my current self to who I was 6 months ago, I see clear improvement—technical knowledge, problem-solving speed, project complexity I can handle. This comparison motivates me to keep going. The second comparison is measurable; the first is endless.

---

## Truth #3

### Truth

Gratitude sharpens decision-making more than ambition alone.

### Evidence from my life

On days when I'm frustrated chasing the next achievement, I make rushed decisions. When I pause to acknowledge what I've already built, my mind clears. Gratitude makes me humble enough to listen to feedback and calm enough to think strategically instead of react emotionally. This calm mindset has led to better career moves than pure drive.

# Assignment 4. Have you ever cut corners (unethical / dishonest / shortcut behavior — not necessarily illegal)? If yes, how did it make you feel?

### Important

You don't need to write the full story.

Focus on the feeling:

* guilt
* fear
* shame
* stress
* regret
* numbness
* etc.

This is about self-awareness, not judgment.

### Answer Format

Yes

If Yes:

**What emotion did you feel?** (minimum 50–100 words)

## Answer

Answer: Yes

What emotion did you feel?

Guilt mixed with relief, which was confusing. At first, guilt dominated—I felt like I was letting my family down, being selfish, not showing up when I was expected. I worried about what relatives would say or think. There was this weight of obligation I'd carried my whole life without questioning it.

But underneath the guilt was something else: stress lifting. When I stopped forcing myself to attend, I realized how much energy those gatherings drained—not just time, but mental energy from dealing with negativity and discouragement. The guilt whispered "you should feel bad about this," but my body felt lighter.

What stayed with me most was the regret about the years I wasted before making this choice. I wished I'd set the boundary earlier. The guilt faded over time as I saw that limiting my time at gatherings didn't make me a bad person—it made me clearer, healthier, and actually more present with my immediate family when I did show up.

The real lesson: guilt isn't always a sign you did something wrong. Sometimes it's just the cost of changing old patterns that never actually served you.

Week 1 Reflection – Self-Paced Learning

 

I finished DevOps Micro Internship Cohort 2 and decided to keep going. No structured program this time, just self-paced learning, but with the same discipline.

 

By Wednesday, I realized something.

 

My phone was winning.

 

I'd start a learning session, focused and determined, and 15 minutes in, an Instagram notification. Then a message. Then "just checking" the news. Two hours would disappear. I'd panic and tell myself,costs "I'll catch up later," but later never came.

 

That's when it hit me: I don't have a learning problem. I have a distraction problem.

 

This week, I watched myself slip into old patterns. Social media scrolling. Checking messages every few minutes. Telling myself I'd have 4 solid learning hours, but actually having 4 fragmented ones. Each fragment momentum, andcosting me focus, momentum, clarity.

 

The insight wasn't dramatic. It was simple: I need protected time to build myself properly. Not more time. Protected time.

 

So I made a decision. No notifications. No scrolling. Just me and the learning experience as fundamentals.

 

Small systems compound. Not perfection. Not motivation. Systems.

 

I was coming from a banking background with zero technical experience 3 years ago. Now I'm deep into DevOps. It's not magic. It's consistency protected from distraction.

 

Week 1 taught me the transformation isn't about working harder. It's about working smarter by eliminating the noise.
